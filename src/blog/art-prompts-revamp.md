In the past year, I've revisited my largest personal project, Art Prompts, and gave it a complete revamp. As is always the case with these projects, it took significantly longer than I expected, but I've finally released the revamped version in late October 2025. In order to document this journey and share some of my thoughts, I wanted to write a quick case study for the revamp (spoiler alert: it did not end up being a "quick" case study). This article won't go into a huge amount of technical details, rather focusing on the philosophical choices I made throughout the year. The main tenet of this revamp was sustainability: this new version should be able to run without any of my input for months or even years.

![A screenshot of the homepage of Art Prompts, containing a large text saying 'Draw a four-leaf clover with raindrops on it.' Below the text is a 'New prompt' button and several illustrated buttons standing for various categories of prompts.](/assets/img/screenshots/screenshot-artprompts.png)

## Context & short history
Back in December 2019, I coded my first ever personal project: an Android app called Art Prompts that allowed artists to get ideas for what to draw. It was a simple app, consisting of the main menu, where users could choose which category they wanted to open, and then the prompts screen, where they could click a button and get one randomly selected prompt from my database. I built the app on top of Firebase, Google's app development backend platform. This allowed me to set up the database within Firestore and just focus on the front-end, which was, for a baby programmer, more than enough to worry about.

With the help of some friends, I came up with 600 prompts, a hundred for each of the categories in the app (Animals, People, Places, Fantasy, Sci-fi, and Horror), and I hired one of my artist friends, [Martin Sedlák](https://martise.art/), to quickly draw up illustrations for each of the categories. Overall, the app took me a few weeks take from initial idea to release on Google Play, in the second half of January 2020.

This marked the beginning of a journey that's being ongoing for half a decade and probably will be ongoing for twice as long more.

The app got relatively popular relatively fast, gaining a couple thousand active users. I figured since I already had the prompts, I might as well do something else fun with them, so I also coded a Discord bot that would draw on this same database and let users get prompts directly in Discord. I posted about this bot on Reddit and it pretty much immediately got invited into 100 Discord servers. I also made my own Discord server, originally to offer support for this bot, but eventually to just host a community of artists around the project in general. Over the following two years, the community grew so much that for a while, Art Prompts was my main source of income. Admittedly, it wasn't a large income, but it, combined with the genuinely lovely and vibrant community, kept me motivated to keep working on the project.

I kept expanding features of the app, eventually adding a Premium category called Myths & Legends that users could buy for around $10. In the midst of working on the Android app and the Discord bot, I also hired another friend of mine, [Vladislav Bagnyuk ](https://vladislavbagnyuk.com/), to code a website and a web app for Art Prompts. At that point, however, the project was already fairly scattered -- there was the Android app, the Discord bot, eventually also a Twitch bot, a newsletter, the Discord community, and now this new web app. The website tried to allow users to orientate themselves in this network of projects, but ended up being more of a series of links and short descriptions rather than a proper hub for all things Art Prompts.

As you might expect, in due time, even though the project kept growing and growing, I burned out. Having spent most of my free time for several years working on Art Prompts, and having to worry about getting through university and pulling my weight at my job, I could no longer keep up with the demands of this ever-expanding project. This wouldn't have been a problem if the project was designed to not break, but, because it was my first-ever large project and I was pretty bad at project management, this was not the case. Art Prompts needed constant maintenance, updates, and moderation. This, of course, was unsustainable in the long-term. I mostly left the project alone, taking the much-needed break.

Within a few months, the Android app got removed from Play Store by Google because a lack of frequent updates that would ensure the app met Google's ever-changing policies. The community stayed very invested in the project, and the Discord server stayed safe thanks to its amazing moderators, but without any new updates, eventually even the Discord server mostly died down.

This is where I found myself in late 2024: you're all caught up.

## The revamp
Obviously, this would've been a lackluster ending for the project and I wasn't very happy with it. During my break, I kept thinking about Art Prompts and eventually, I decided to tackle the project again, from scratch, using the skills I've accrued over the 6 years of my software engineering experience. The idea for the revamped version was simple: design everything in such a way that, were I to take another break for a year or even two, the project would not break in my absence. All of the large and small design decisions made over the course of the project were made with this core idea in mind.

One major decision was to move the project away from as many third-party services as possible. This was mostly prompted by Google's takedown of the Art Prompts Android app -- this made me recognize how fickle and demanding relying on third parties can be. For the project to be sustainable in the long-term without needing constant input, it needed to stand on its own as much as possible. Because of this, I scrapped the Firebase connection and implemented my own database in MySQL on my VPS, and implemented my own auth system (which was previously also ran through Firebase).

Early on in the process, I thought about the various branches of the old project and how to best unify them. I knew I didn't want to try and revive the Android app, as that would mean becoming subject to Google's whims again, but I also knew I didn't want to scrap the Discord bot, which was incredibly popular even after the years of no updates, having been invited to over 7 000 Discord servers. After some thought, I came to the conclusion of making one central API around one central database, on which the new branches would be build. The Android app and the old web app would get replaced by the new web app, which would be coded as a Progressive Web App (PWA) and thus could be installed on phones to provide a native-like experience for users (within limits). The new website would be centered around this web app rather than having it as a minor feature like the old website did. The Discord bot would get revamped to use this new API as well, and because of this could eventually have some new fun features I will talk more about below. The briefly aforementioned Twitch bot could use this API as well. Given the fact that these ran without any problems for the entire duration of my absence, I am fairly confident that the revamp versions will need very minimal maintenance. Other that this, I was (and continue to be) very cautious when deciding to add a new branch to the project in order to not blow the scope of the project out of proportion again.

And with these decisions in mind, I started work on the project. After some research, I chose Gatsby as my front-end framework. I like working with React because I find splitting features of the front-end into components very readable and sustainable, and I prefer my websites being static and SSR. For the back-end, I spun up a REST API in Express.js, and a database in MySQL (as its the SQL variation I'm the most used to).

Let's talk about the front-end first, as its arguably the less complex part. As I mentioned, the old website was sort of a mess trying to link people towards the various branches of the project. In order to get from the homepage to the web app, users would have to use the navigation menu (or footer), which would take them to a page about the web app, and then they'd have to click the Open app button in order to actually get to the prompts. Obviously, this is pretty bad UX, considering the prompts were the main thing users would come to the website for. In this new version, the web app takes center stage, being prominently displayed on the homepage before even any information about the project as a whole. Other than this, the design of the website was actually pretty straightforward. I did my best to follow all accessibility guidelines, fix all compilation warnings, and document every page and component via JSDoc in order so, returing to the code after some time, I would have an easier time updating it. As of release, a few of the components still don't have their JSDoc documentation done, but it's high on my priority list.

Now for the back-end, which serves as the real core of the project. There are several features I would like to discuss here because of their interesting design decisions and processes.

The project operates on top of a standalone internal API that I coded using [Express.js](https://expressjs.com/). Having the API be its own service, decoupled from the front-end, allows me to re-use it in other parts of the project, such as an automated Bluesky posting script and the Discord and Twitch bots (both discussed in more detail below). Express is very stable, and I find working with a traditional REST API structure to be much preferable to a lot of full-stack frameworks. It's modular, easily expandable, and simple to maintain.

All of the prompts and other data for the project are stored in my own database on the server where Art Prompts runs. In the old version of the project, this used to be Firebase instead. Moving away from Firebase to MySQL accomplishes a couple of things. Firstly, I get a relational database system, which is, in my experience, a lot more straight-forward to use with the type of data I store as opposed to Firebase's NoSQL, document-based system. My old prompt system was complicated to use, and therefore, while NoSQL is *technically* more scalable, my needs benefit from an SQL approach much more, making it actually the scalable way. By neatly setting up a proper relational database, I'm able to work with powerful (and very fast) SQL queries, ensure data format corectness, and leverage a system I have more experience with (I've worked with relational databases in my professional life more than with NoSQL ones). Secondly, I move away from Google. As I've mentioned, I don't like relying on third-party services much, and especially on Google (related reading: [Collecting Hollow Knight skins: A reflection on a decentralized internet through the lens of a silly 10 GB collection and a month of my free time.](/blog/hollow-knight-skins)). The decision to host my own database allowed me to pay them less money, rely on their technologies and unpredictable decisions less, as well as move user data away from Google thus improving on user privacy. It's a win-win!

When I still had the Android app, Art Prompts used to make some money from ads. The main prompt screen would have an inobstrusive banner ads at the bottom of the screen, and users could choose to watch video ads in order to unlock some exclusive prompts. This approach made a good amount of money for the amount of users I had. I still consider it a vaguely valid approach to monetization from the business side when handled well, but my personal opinion on the ethics of advertising has developed a lot since then. Because of this, I knew going into the revamp that I wanted to avoid ads on the website altogether. Thankfully, even in the old version of Art Prompts, I had set up a different, more ethical approach to monetization: premium categories. The vast majority of the prompts in the project are free, but some special categories require the user to purchase it first. Originally, this was the Myths & Legends category in the Android app, and eventually the History, Architecture, Objects, and NSFW categories in the old web app. The Android app used to run the purchases through Google's in-app billing library (which was mandatory for Android apps at the time), and the web app would use PayPal. In the revamped web app, I included all of these premium categories and I switched payment gates. Again, there were several reasons for this. On the more philosophical hand, I prefered using an EU-based payment gate, as the political situation in the US doesn't seem very stable to trust it with long-term core business third-party services. On the practical hand, an increasing amount of payment gate providers are forbidding any sort of 18+ content (including PayPal and Stripe, another gate I considered using); and the NSFW prompts in Art Prompts often qualify for this, even when they only contain descriptions of sexual situations rather than any actual depictions (oftentimes, this is classified by payment gate providers as "adult literature" and is forbidden). Switching payment gates to GoPay, a Czech-based company which explicitly allows NSFW content, dealt with both of these problems. There was a lot of bureacracy to go through while setting the payment gate up, but the actual technical side of the set up was fairly simple, and the customer support team was very helpful in guiding me through the rest. They also take a much smaller cut than PayPal does.

With this consolidation of the Myths & Legends premium category from the Android app and the other premium categories from the old web app came an interesting problem. I obviously had data on which users bought premium categories in the web app, so I could transfer those data points 1:1 into the new database; however, I didn't have proper data for purchases of the M&L category in the Android app. Google only provided me with some data that wasn't transferrable 1:1 to the new database (and they made it fairly difficult to access this data in a programmatic fashion as well). Because of this, I needed to come up with a system that would allow people who have previously purchased the M&L category to unlock it for themselves in the revamped version of the project. To this end, I created the Claim system, which allows users to input a pre-generated code in order to unlock a specific premium category for their Art Prompts account for free. This system is currently only used for transferring the old M&L categories (which, on the homepage, includes a link to the Claim system page, alongside with an explanation of how to claim it and why this is even necessary), but later on it can be used for any awarding of premium categories for free -- as rewards for suggesting prompts or otherwise helping out with the project, as a part of giveaways, etc.

I talk a lot about moving away from Google in this article, and that was indeed a major part of the revamp. However, one area where I could not move away from it was authentication. The vast majority of the users in the old web app used Google Sign in to create their accounts. This meant that, in order to preserve backwards compatibility, I had to implement Google Auth on the new website as well. I made sure to provide an alternative (e-mail and password login), and I made sure that users that did sign up via Google are able to set up a new password for themselves and later disconnect their Google accounts from their Art Prompts account, should they choose to do so. The implementation of Google Auth was honestly a pain, predominantly because of Google's rapidly changing recommended approaches to implementing it, and their very outdated and convoluted documentation pages. Eventually though, I've managed to implement it using the official `google-auth-library` npm library (and later on, I will be implementing Discord and Twitch OAuth as well; see below). Honestly, while I do understand the convenience and security improvements of single-click sign on, if I had the choice, I would rather avoid implementing this system entirely, as I really don't like relying on Google on anything anymore. But oh well, compromises must be made.

During the entire process of the revamp, I also wrote a fairly extensive error reporting system for myself. I'm using `winston`, a logging library, throughout the project for complex logs. I split all the events in the API into three categories: `info`, `warn`, and `error`. `Info`-level events are basically everything that happens in the API, and they are simply logged into a text file. `Warn`-level events are user-facing issues that are usually caused by the user -- in other words, most 4xx errors -- think the user inputting the incorrect password, or trying to register an account that already exists. These errors are logged into a separate file and reported to the user using an error message that describes the problem and the potential solution in a human-friendly way. Finally, `error`-level events are problems that were most likely caused by a bug in my code, i.e. 5xx errors. These are (mostly) the only problems I need to actually worry about fixing. They are handled the same was as `warn` events, but they are further logged into an `errors` database table with extra data, for which I then have a dashboard where I can look through the errors and figure out how to fix them.

Of course, during this process I kept an extensive documentation of everything I was doing. I briefly considered using a system like [wiki.js](https://js.wiki/) (which I would wholeheartedly recommend for keeping an external documentation, I use it for my homelab and for one job project), but eventually I decided to just write my own documentation as a part of the website. I placed them in administrator-only parts of the website (that I will also briefly mention below). I started with only a single page, but as the development progressed, I eventually decided to split it into multiple pages, as the single page was getting too cluttered. I used this page to document the project as a whole, various choices I made when developing the website, as well as the marketing strategy, future plans, etc. The code itself I extensively documented using JSDoc, a tool I wasn't super closely familiar before this project -- I'm glad I learned something new, because after writing documentation for all of the routes and functions in the API, I definitely see the appeal of this system. It's standardized, pretty simple to use, and VSCode knows how to automatically read it and show hints and auto-complete suggestions based on it. I haven't documented all of the React components in the front-end this way yet, but having done so in another project at work, I can say I will definitely be doing that in Art Prompts as well. Overall a great tool: if you haven't heard of JSDoc before, or are only lightly acquinted with it, I would definitely recommend reading up.

In the old version, working with the database of prompts was very clunky. I had the prompts stored in Firebase, a NoSQL document-based database, in batches only of only loosely organized prompts. Obviously, for data such as my prompts, this is not ideal, and over the years of working on Art Prompts I've come up with a few systems, and even coded a few small tools, to make this process easier for me (for example, a Google Sheets script to pick some unposted prompts and format them into the correct format for the database structure I was using). When I made the switch to SQL, this got a lot easier, but still, writing SQL queries or using database management software (such as [DBeaver](https://dbeaver.io/), the FOSS tool I use) every time I want to add or edit prompts, find out how many users the project currently has, or check up on the status of a purchase, is annoying and heavy-handed. Furthermore, if the project ever grew enough that more people would join the team, expecting non-developers to be digging around in a database directly like that is awkward and more importantly unsafe. For these reasons, I decided to implement some admin-only dashboards for Art Prompts. These are pages and API routes that only admin accounts have access to and allow me to lists users, prompts, purchases, and everything else in neat little tables, along with statistics, and sometimes actions that can be taken on each individual row (editing a prompt, adding a role to a user, etc.). Since these features very much aren't critical to the project, I didn't fully finish any of them before releasing the project; in fact, a few months later they still aren't finished, and I'm only slowly adding functionalities to them when I have time and feel like it. My eventual goal is to be able to work with the Art Prompts database only through the website in 99% of cases, and only have to touch the database directly for troubleshooting or for major changes such as adding a fully new category of prompts (which is something that will only happen like once a year or so, and requires code changes to the website anyways, so it's fine that that has to be done manually).

As a habit built from my freelance work, I track my time working on any project with fairly detailed records. Thanks to that, I can say that the entire revamp, start to release, took me around 440 hours of work. I started working on the revamp at the start of November 2024 and released the website in late October 2025. Here's how the release went!

## Release
In order to prepare for the release date, I asked a few of my friends to help me test the website. I wrote out around 15 testing scenarios, ranging from simple things like getting prompts from a specific category, tests that would help me personally prepare for production scenarios (such as asking for a GDPR-compliant record of all personal data for a given user), to more complicated scenarios like creating an account, buying a Premium category, including explicit categories in the *All prompts* category, and claiming the Premium Myths & Legends category for free using an order ID from the old Android app. These tests were ran and recorded by multiple people using many different devices and browsers. I went through the recordings and did short interviews with everyone to see if they found any bugs or common friction points in the UX. After analyzing all of this, I fixed a few of the major problems that came up, and wrote down the rest as TODOs for the future.

On the day of release, I had to transfer all of the user data from Firebase to my SQL database, both from Firebase Auth and Firebase Firestore. Google does not make this simple, as it doesn't offer any comprehensive simple way to export the data from their ecosystem. I assume that this is to create friction for people trying to leave.

After that was done, I changed the webserver settings to point the production domain to the new website, and set up the API to run on the production server. I went through all of the testing scenarios I wrote before again myself, just to check if everything was working properly. After I made sure everything was working, I made an announcement on the Art Prompts Discord server and on social media.

Thanks to the Discord announcement, there was immediately a fairly large influx of users on the website, which was a great way to further test if everything worked. This trial by fire found that almost everything was working no problem, except an occasional issue in Google Auth (reported by the aforementioned error reporting system) that popped up once every fifty login attempts or so. I eventually managed to fix it a few weeks later (hopefully), partly thanks to this [relatively detailed writeup](https://markaicode.com/debugging-oauth-authentication-flows-complete-guide/).

As a part of this revamp, I had to make the difficult decision to sunset the community Discord server. In the previous years when I was actively working on Art Prompts, it hosted a very vibrant and genuinely awesome community of artists who were excited about the project and oftentimes helped out with it, but as I burned out and stopped working on the project, the server more or less died (although it stayed clean and safe thanks to the amazing work of its moderators). I thought about this decision for a long time, but running a whole community that I need to be very active in to keep it alive just isn't sustainable under the assumption that I might need to take a hiatus at any point (especially when it comes to being social, which I don't feel like relatively often and regularly). At some point I might consider creating a forum or something similar to that, but it would have to be at a point where I'd be able to hire someone at least part-time to moderate it and make sure the community stays active and vibrant. At this stage of the project, I announced that the Discord community server will get deleted in April, and set it to read-only. That way, community members can back up any personally important messages that accumulated there over the years. Furthermore, to thank everyone who was suggesting prompts in the community (several hundred of the prompts in the database were suggested by the community, alongside probably thousands more that people suggested but I didn't end up adding for one reason or another), I compiled a spreadsheet of everyone who suggested anything that got accepted into the project, and I'm slowly reaching out to them in DMs to offer them free access to the Premium categories.

Overall, the release day went pretty much as well as I could've hoped. I gave it a few days, partly to take a break, and partly to truly make sure everything worked, and then I started thinking about marketing and my future plans for the project.

## Marketing
Given extensive testing and feedback from users and community members, I am fairly confident in having made a good website that people like using. As with all things on the internet, though, that's only one part of the process; the other part is actually letting people know that it exists. For this, I did some research and consultations, and I came up with a marketing strategy that I wanted to implement and then revisit every 3 months or so. Same as with the website itself, my priority goal was to automate as much as possible, to make sure everything would be running smoothly even if I needed to take a hiatus from the project. With marketing, this isn't as doable as with the codebase, but I ended up finding out that a lot of social media stuff can be either fully or at least semi-automated. I started implementing the following strategy in November 2025 and I'm planning to ramp up its intensity in the first few months of 2026.

For social media marketing, I knew I wanted to avoid platforms that I have ideological issues with, such as Twitter or Facebook. Ideally, I would like to avoid Instagram as well, but because of its prominence among artists, I eventually decided to include it in my efforts. Other than Instagram, I also decided to post on Bluesky (as the popular alternative to Twitter), Pinterest (which proved itself very useful for me when marketing my worldbuilding blog, Eledris), and Tumblr (as the one social media platform where I'm actually personally active on). I was thinking of also doing something on a more open-source federated platform, such as Mastodon, but eventually I decided to skip that for now because I'm not super familiar with the culture there. I might reconsider this when I'm revisiting the marketing strategy in April 2026.

Most of the social media posts would include individual prompts. To make sure that people actually have incentive to visit the website, I decided to limit myself to posting at most 20% of all free prompts on any one social media platform, and 10% of the Premium ones. Doing some math on how many prompts I have and how many prompts I was planning on posting, I figured that I could reasonably post one prompt a day to social media, with one a week being Premium, without running out of prompts to post for around two years. After that, I'll need to figure out something different :)

On Tumblr, I created a new blog for Art Prompts, [artprompts-app](https://www.tumblr.com/blog/artprompts-app), on which I posted some basic information about the project and then started posting one prompt a day. Tumblr has a queue feature, which allowed me to schedule a bunch of prompts in advance, and then let Tumblr automatically post them at a given point of time every day. To plan which prompts I want to post, I just wrote a quick SQL query to select random prompts and filled out a spreadsheet. Eventually, I think this could be automated further by using the Tumblr API, or implementing some sort of an Admin dashboard for planning out posts on the Art Prompts website, but for now, this is good enough.

On Bluesky, I also created a new account for Art Prompts and I used the domain name to claim the very memorable username, [@artprompts.app](https://bsky.app/profile/artprompts.app). Bluesky has a very extensive and easy-to-use API, so I was able to fully automate posting there using a simple script and a cronjob on the server. I might write a separate article on implementing that, as it was honestly a very fun tiny project for a day of work. Besides these daily prompts, I also followed the accounts of a few artists and I'm reposting (i.e. retweeting) their art semi-regularly (when I feel like scrolling through a Twitter-like app looking at cool art).

I already had an account on Instagram for Art Prompts, but as a part of the revamp I slightly rebranded it to [@artprompts.app](https://www.instagram.com/artprompts.app/) and I fully reworked the strategy for posting there. Originally, I was posting in a very specific pattern: one community-submitted art and then two posts of a prompt. On the old grid, this looked really good and made the profile look very cohesive and sleek. Since Instagram changed how the grid works and ruined that look, I decided to switch gears. After some brainstorming, I settled on just posting prompts on the main profile feed and reposting all of the community art into Stories and making a Highlight out of them. This way, all of the old community art is still accessible in an aesthetic way, but it doesn't break the cohesion of the grid (and won't break it again if Instagram changes anything again). Furthermore, if and when someone tags the account in their art inspired by one of the prompts, I can very easily share that post into the page's Stories; this should heavily simplify the process of doing this, as before I would have to download the image, re-upload it again as a post, and tag the original artist in the post and its description. For the actual posts, the Meta Business Suite allows you to schedule posts up to a month in advance -- not as far ahead as I'd like (ideally I would like to schedule them 3 months in advance), but still good enough that I can just revisit it once every few weeks and schedule everything for the upcoming period.

Finally, Pinterest. This is a different platform from the other ones, as its kind of more of a search engine than a social media site. I used Pinterest before to great success on my worldbuilding blog (in fact, in its early stages most of my traffic went from there, until my articles started ranking high on Google), so I had some experience with the platform. Nevertheless, it's been a few years since I last used it actively, so I still had to do some research. Back with the old version of Art Prompts, I used to post a few of the prompts as Pins under my personal account. After the revamp, I elected to create a separate account for [Art Prompts](https://pinterest.com/artprompts_app/), to make the posts a bit more organized (at the price of losing the followers and algorithm reputation of my personal account). First, I made boards for each of the categories of prompts, as well as one *All prompts* board, and then I posted a couple of prompts into each of them, just so the profile wouldn't be completely empty. Pinterest lets you schedule posts up to a month in advance, so then I scheduled random prompt posts for the rest of the month, and I need to manually reschedule new ones each month. There are tools to get around this and schedule much more in advance (I have some fairly positive experience with [Tailwind](https://www.tailwindapp.com/), but it's been several years since I worked with it, so I can't speak to its quality now), but I didn't want to rely on third-party software that I might have to pay a subscription for. Same as on Bluesky, from time to time I pin art inspiration posts on Pinterest to an *Art Inspiration* board, both to genuinely create a nice library of inspiration, as well as to please the algorithm a bit.

For Instagram and Pinterest, image-centered platforms, I coded a small tool within the Admin-only API to generate images with the prompts automatically. In my Admin Prompt dashboard, I can simply click on a button and it downloads the image, created using a template I designed in [Penpot](https://penpot.app/) (self-hosted Figma alternative). Using `sharp`, the API inserts the text of the prompt in the correct font and guesstimates the correct size based on the prompt's character count (using a dictionary of `characterCount`:`fontSize` that I just figured out via some trial-and-error; after using it on around 300 prompts, I haven't ran into a problem with this approach yet). I had a similar system coded back in the old version of Art Prompts, but there it was a standalone `npm` project with a simple script. I still don't dislike that approach, but given that getting social media graphics from prompts is a pretty common thing I need to do, and counting with the option of eventually getting someone else to handle social media if Art Prompts ever gets more popular, this non-technical-friendly approach felt smarter.

Besides social media marketing, I also want to eventually reach out to some influencers. I had a very positive experience during the old version of the project with reaching out to smaller YouTubers and asking them for feedback and if they'd be up for making a video using the app -- they get a content idea, the app gets some promotion, and usually I offered them access to any of the Premium categories they wanted for free, as well as sometimes a giveaway of one of them for their viewers. Every time a video like this got made, the old app got a little boost of downloads, and the videos ended up being interesting for the YouTubers' viewers as well -- another win-win scenario. To this end, I used to have a compiled huge spreadsheet of YouTube channels I could reach out to, sorted by subscriber count and date of last upload. After releasing the revamp and thinking about the marketing strategy, I dug this spreadsheet out and starting slowly updating it, removing any influences who haven't been active for a few months, updating the subscriber counts, and also adding a section for Instagram influencers; eventually, I want to look for some people I could reach out to directly on Instagram, but in the meantime, as I'm updating the YouTube sheet, I found that a lot of the people stopped posting on YouTube but are still active on Instagram in the intervening years, so it's useful to save their information for later use anyways. As of January 2026, I haven't reached out to anyone with the new version of the project yet, but I'd like to try at least a few people before April.

Finally, as I've been getting involved with my local community in my town (a medium-sized city in the Czech Republic), especially in queer and performing arts circles, I plan on reaching out to some community organizations here to ask them if they'd like to do some little collaborations, ranging from just reposting each other's content on social media to more organized events. I'd also like to have some fliers printed out and put up at my local art universities, community spaces, and art-centered events.

I also tried doing a bit of paid marketing, and I have complicated opinions on it. Specifically, I tried putting about $20 into Tumblr Blaze and another $10 into Instagram ads. My first problem is ideological: especially with Meta, I don't feel super good giving them extra money, plus I'm generally very anti-ad and use ad-blockers pretty much everywhere, so it does feel a bit hypocritical to be running ads. I don't have that much of a problem with Tumblr in this regard, because their Blaze system cannot be targeted very highly (thus alleviating a lot of the privacy concerns around Meta products), and because I actually like the platform (for the most part). As far as efficacy goes, both of my experiments lead to increased engagement on the social media profiles, the Instagram ad also lead to traffic to the site and a few registered users. None of the campaigns lead to any Premium category purchases, but it's possible that it just had too small of a budget for that. I might think about trying an experiment with giving more money into it, but I'll need to think about the aforementioned ethics of this beforehand.

A lot of my work early after release of the project was around the aforementioned Admin-only tools. I implemented dashboards for all the prompts, categories, users, languages (see below), errors, claim codes (for claiming Premium categories for free), purchases, roles, third-party auth integrations, social media posts, and service workers (see below as well). A lot of these dashboards follow a very similar structure of loading all the data from an Admin-only API route and then displaying it in a table, alongside with some statistics and basic filtering. In many of the dashboards, I also implemented actions, such as adding a record for a social media post or deactivating a user straight from the table. The vast majority of these actions are still unimplemented, and they are one of the things I would like to look at in the first few months of 2026.

Right before the end of the year, I also implemented a system that lets me schedule new prompts in advance. I can simply set the creation date of a prompt to the future, and that prompt happily lives in the database, safe and backed up, unavailable to regular users until that specific date passes. This let me schedule new weekly prompts for months in advance, and helped take out a lot of the regular manual labor previously involved in Art Prompts.

Finally, at the time of writing this, I am currently working on implementing Discord oAuth in order to connect the website with the Discord bot. More on that in the following section, as I'm not done implementing it yet.

## Future plans
I think I've successfully achieved my intial goal: the website runs and barring any major unforseen circumstances, it should keep running without me needing to touch it for months at a time. Nevertheless, I actually currently have the time and energy to work on it more, so I have a lot of plans I would like to implement over the following months.

Two small parts of the Art Prompts project are the Discord and Twitch bots. In the old version, these obviously ran using the Firebase database as well, and particularly the Discord bot got incredibly popular (being invited to around 8 000 servers as of the time of writing). Because of this, I definitely don't want to scrap the bot, but I do want to rewrite it so it's more maintainable, uses the new API, and has some new features I wanted to implement even back then. The Twitch bot is also on my list to revamp, but since that wasn't as popular as the Discord bot, it's less of a priority for me (I do still think it could be a really fun tool for art streamers though, and it shouldn't be very difficult to code and run).

The revamp of the Discord bot is surprisingly fairly complicated. If I wanted just a 1:1 copy that uses the new API, it would be relatively simple, but I want to implement new features that make the bot easier to use and allows users to get use out of more Art Prompts features. One major thing like this is users being able to grant access to Premium categories they have purchased to a Discord server of their choosing. Other planned features include turning off explicit categories for a specific server, or choosing a channel on a server into which the bot should send random daily prompts (especially this last one is heavily requested by the community). All of these features individually sound fairly simple, but obviously if you think about it for a bit, it's actually a complex problem. In order for it to work safely, the website needs Discord authentication, and then needs to hit the Discord API to get a list of the user's servers and keep track of each server's settings. The user also needs a space to view and edit these settings. Because of this, I'm implementing a Discord bot dashboard for the website, where users logged in via Discord will be able to edit the bot's settings for all servers they moderate, as well as grant Premium category access to categories they've purchased to any Discord server they are in. As a part of this, the bot also needs to keep a database of all the Discord servers it's in alongside their individual settings. I'm currently in the process of implementing this, but I'm guesstimating it should be about a month or two of (admittedly casual) work.

The same goes for the Twitch bot, for which I'd like to create a similar dashboard, where streamers could edit settings for the bot on their channel, and users could donate access to Premium categories to their favorite streamers. I haven't thought about how to implement this exact feature yet, as implementing the new Twitch bot is months down the line.

Finally, one major feature I want to implement is localization. In the old Android app, we had a French version that one of the community members helped create, and this had a great response from the French-speaking community. I would like to implement an i18n system for the website, as well as for all the prompts (and eventually the Discord and Twitch bots as well), and translate it to French, probably Portuguese (given that a large percentage of the Art Prompts user base is from Brazil), as well as Czech (mostly because I have some friends who are junior translators and could use the experience, money, and project for their portfolio). This will obviously be a lot of work both for me as the developer and for other people as the translators, so I'm only planning this in the later months of this year. Here's hoping!
